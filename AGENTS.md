## Starting point

This workspace starts with Pietro Miotti’s account of finding archaeological features in public airborne LiDAR data. The local project contains this guide and the documentation under `docs/`. The research question, study area, and implementation will follow from the owner’s idea. [Miotti’s article, updated 11 November 2026](https://pietromiotti.com/latest/maya-site/)

## How the reference project worked

1. **Find usable coverage.** Public survey coverage led Miotti to the Maya lowlands. His team rendered bare-earth images from NASA LiDAR flight strips covering about 1,200 km², revealing terrain beneath the forest canopy.
2. **Screen the images.** Forty-eight AI agents followed a shared visual guide. They looked for arrangements resembling plazas, platforms, and pyramids and considered lookalikes such as hills, sinkholes, and roads.
3. **Check the terrain.** At one hilltop, cross-sections through the original point cloud showed ground returns tracing a raised mound and adjoining level surfaces below the canopy. The proposed uses of those features remained hypotheses.
4. **Check prior mapping.** The agents searched publications and archives. Miotti reported the candidate to Mexico’s National Institute of Anthropology and History (INAH), supplying its coordinates in a confidential annex. INAH found no entry at that location. Miotti’s November correction credits Estrada-Belli and colleagues with mapping the site in 2025. [Corrected account](https://pietromiotti.com/latest/maya-site/) · [Prior mapping study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jasrep.2025.105288)

## Applying the approach

- Once the question is defined, choose one survey strip with a feature that can be checked independently. Keep its dataset identifier and rendering settings with the image so another person can reproduce the view.
- For each candidate, record the visible geometry, plausible alternative explanations, and the raw-data check. Keep proposed dates and uses explicitly tentative.
- For a claim of novelty in Portugal, check Endovélico first and record relevant CNS identifiers, the date checked, bibliography and source coverage. Compare the candidate’s location with published feature maps as well as registry records. Record which sources cover the location and what each shows. For the Maya example, the 2025 study identifies a public GIS collection for that comparison. [Study and data availability](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X25003219)

## Local documentation

- **Trees → Filter NDVI proxy** adds an inclusive Min/Max range in `[-1, 1]`, combined with the existing height threshold. It works with both height and NDVI colours, in the map and 3D. When Tree clusters is enabled, each crown passes or fails by its median proxy value, and its member samples use that same median for the range decision. Unassigned vegetation and crowns without a valid median are excluded. At detailed views without clusters, each sample uses its own valid index. The wide crown overview uses crown medians. Empty, reversed or out-of-range bounds are reported and hide filtered vegetation until corrected. Changing either bound clears the selection. The filtered crown count, map/3D selection and GPU visibility must use the same inclusive criterion. `vegetation-filters.js` holds shared range checks. Run `node scripts/check-vegetation-filters.cjs` for combined height/range checks, missing values, inclusive boundaries and real-data counts. NDVI remains an exploratory 8-bit colour proxy with unverified reflectance calibration.

- **Current parish vegetation dataset:** [surface.html](docs/aldeia-da-mata/surface.html) covers Aldeia da Mata’s 37.28 km² CAOP2025 parish. Processing decoded all hierarchy levels in 60 cached DGT files (1,083,339,788 source returns), retaining 165,576,602 class 3–5 returns with supported ground and normalized height 0.1–60 m. The prepared data has 14,623,653 occupied 1 m canopy cells, 7,323,951 reduced display points and 250,323 estimated crowns. Canopy cells retain the highest measured return without filling gaps. Display points use deterministic 1-in-5 selection of 1 m horizontal / 1 m height voxels. Normalization uses the existing CAOP-masked 0.5 m terrain tiles. Species and current tree condition are not inferred.

- **Parish clustering and IDs:** `scripts/tree-clustering-sparse.cjs` segments the entire measured canopy before cutting display tiles. It retains the village algorithm’s 3×3 weighted smoothing, >=3 m peaks, height-dependent 3–6 m seed separation, eight-neighbour height-aware growing, <=12 m radius and >=4 occupied cells per crown. A sparse measured-value array, global cell lookup, uint32 labels and compact priority heap support parish-wide processing. No tile-local segmentation or halo reconciliation is used. All 11,315 crowns crossing display-tile edges retain one global ID. IDs are exact integers below 2²⁴ in WebGL float attributes and are dataset-specific; they differ from the earlier village IDs. Crown area is occupied 1 m cells and height is the highest accepted measured return. Sparse crowns, touching crowns and peaks within 12 m of the CAOP boundary are flagged. These are candidate crowns, not a verified tree inventory; adjoining trees may merge/split and concealed understory trees may be missed.

- **Parish NDVI proxy:** All 60 source files passed numerical RGB/NIR availability checks.
