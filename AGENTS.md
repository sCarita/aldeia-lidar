# LiDAR project guide

## Starting point

This workspace starts with Pietro Miotti’s account of finding archaeological features in public airborne LiDAR data. The local project currently consists of this guide. The research question, study area, and implementation will follow from the owner’s idea. [Miotti’s article, updated 11 November 2026](https://pietromiotti.com/latest/maya-site/)

## How the reference project worked

1. **Find usable coverage.** Public survey coverage led Miotti to the Maya lowlands. His team rendered bare-earth images from NASA LiDAR flight strips covering about 1,200 km², revealing terrain beneath the forest canopy.
2. **Screen the images.** Forty-eight AI agents followed a shared visual guide. They looked for arrangements resembling plazas, platforms, and pyramids and considered lookalikes such as hills, sinkholes, and roads.
3. **Check the terrain.** At one hilltop, cross-sections through the original point cloud showed ground returns tracing a raised mound and adjoining level surfaces below the canopy. The proposed uses of those features remained hypotheses.
4. **Check prior mapping.** The agents searched publications and archives. Miotti reported the candidate to Mexico’s National Institute of Anthropology and History (INAH), supplying its coordinates in a confidential annex. INAH found no entry at that location. Miotti’s November correction credits Estrada-Belli and colleagues with mapping the site in 2025. [Corrected account](https://pietromiotti.com/latest/maya-site/) · [Prior mapping study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jasrep.2025.105288)

## Applying the approach

- Once the question is defined, choose one survey strip with a feature that can be checked independently. Keep its dataset identifier and rendering settings with the image so another person can reproduce the view.
- For each candidate, record the visible geometry, plausible alternative explanations, and the raw-data check. Keep proposed dates and uses explicitly tentative.
- For a claim of novelty, compare the candidate’s location with published feature maps as well as registry records. Record which sources cover the location and what each shows. For the Maya example, the 2025 study identifies a public GIS collection for that comparison. [Study and data availability](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X25003219)
