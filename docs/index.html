Beneath the canopy

Airborne LiDAR maps a forest from its highest branches to the soil below. Explore the echoes that turn a strip of woodland into a three-dimensional map.

Collecting a map from a moving drone

The drone flies parallel strips while its scanner sweeps sideways. Each sweep measures a new row of the landscape. Overlapping strips help cover the area and align the measurements.

For every echo, the system combines laser distance and beam direction with the drone’s synchronized GNSS position and inertial orientation. Processing corrects the trajectory and aligns the strips into one point cloud. Ground classification then identifies likely soil returns.

The animation shows a simplified survey over the same simulated forest. Real pulses and echoes travel much faster, and canopy gaps determine which pulses reach the soil.

From light to landscape

EMIT

Send a short pulse

A scanner on an aircraft sweeps laser pulses across its flight path. Each pulse illuminates a small patch of the landscape.

RECEIVE

Time the echoes

Leaves, branches and soil scatter light back. Distance equals light speed times round-trip travel time, divided by two. A 1 μs round trip puts the reflecting surface about 150 m away.

LOCATE

Place each return

Combine the distance with the beam direction and the aircraft’s position and orientation. Each echo becomes a point in three dimensions. Millions together describe a surface.

Read the forest in profile

Some light finds gaps between leaves and reaches the soil. In this slice, green points trace vegetation and amber points follow the ground. Move the scan position above to sample another part of the ridge.

Ground classification uses the shape of nearby terrain to identify likely soil returns. Interpolating between those points produces a bare-earth model. Where few pulses reach the ground, the surface is more uncertain.
